What you are seeing

A gate dielectric under a strong field slowly accumulates defects (traps), generated at random positions. Each trap lets electrons hop a little way. On its own a trap does little; nearby traps together give stress-induced leakage current (SILC) that creeps up with time. When a continuous chain of traps finally connects gate to channel, current runs through it and the oxide breaks down.

Because trap positions are random, the breakdown time is a random variable. Its distribution is Weibull:

F(t) = 1 − exp(−(t/η)β)  ⇒  ln(−ln(1−F)) = β ln t − β ln η

so on a Weibull plot the samples fall on a straight line of slope β. Percolation predicts β roughly proportional to oxide thickness: a thin oxide needs only a short chain, so a few unlucky traps are enough. Its failures come earlier and are spread more widely.

Try this in class

  1. Thickness 5.4 nm. Press Stress one device and watch traps accumulate until a path forms. Note the leakage jump.
  2. Run it again: a different breakdown time. Ask the class why.
  3. Press Test 300 devices. The points line up on the Weibull plot; read the slope β.
  4. Change to 2 nm and test 300 again. The new line is shallower (smaller β) and sits to the left. Discuss what that means for a chip with a billion gates.
  5. Increase the area (more columns) at fixed thickness: the line shifts left with the same slope — weakest-link scaling.

Check yourself

Why does a larger gate area fail sooner?Breakdown happens at the first path anywhere in the area. More area means more chances, so the time to the first failure falls. For a Weibull distribution, t63% scales as (area)−1/β.
Why does a shallow Weibull slope (small β) worry a reliability engineer?Products are specified at tiny failure fractions (parts per million). The lifetime at F = 10⁻⁶ is extrapolated down the Weibull line; a shallow line puts that point much earlier than the median, so the usable lifetime shrinks drastically.
Thin SiO₂ breakdowns are often "soft". What does that mean?In ultra-thin oxides the first percolation path often carries only a limited current: the leakage jumps but the transistor may still work. Repeated soft breakdowns and progressive growth of the path eventually lead to hard failure.

The model behind it

A cell-based percolation model (after Degraeve et al., 1995). The dielectric is a grid of cells about 0.9 nm across: n = tox/0.9 nm layers deep and a chosen number of columns wide. Each cell becomes a trap at an independent, exponentially distributed random time. Traps in the same or neighbouring columns of adjacent layers connect. Breakdown is the moment a connected chain spans all layers, found with a union–find over the cells in order of trap time.

Leakage is drawn as direct tunnelling plus a SILC term proportional to trap density, with a jump of four decades at breakdown.

Time is in arbitrary units; trap generation rate depends strongly on field and temperature, which set the absolute lifetime. The model reproduces the statistics, not a specific dielectric.
