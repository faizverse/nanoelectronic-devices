What you are seeing

The gate makes the channel. Below threshold the gate only pushes holes away, leaving a depletion region of fixed negative charge. Above VT it pulls a thin layer of electrons to the surface — the inversion layer — joining source to drain. Its charge per unit area is Cox(VGS − VT − V(x)), where V(x) is the channel voltage at that point.

The drain drives the current. At small VDS the channel is uniform and the device is a resistor controlled by the gate (linear region). As VDS rises, the channel voltage near the drain rises and the gate–channel voltage there falls, so the layer thins toward the drain. When VDS = VGS − VT the charge at the drain end reaches zero: the channel is pinched off and the current saturates.

Watch the electrons: the current is the same at every point along the channel, so where the layer is thin the electrons must move faster. That is continuity, and it is why they race through the pinch-off region.

Try this in class

  1. Set VDS = 0.1 V and raise VGS from 0. Nothing flows until the inversion layer appears at VT = 0.5 V; then current grows linearly with VGS.
  2. Fix VGS = 1.5 V and raise VDS. Watch the layer taper and the operating point move along the curve. Ask the class to predict the VDS at which it pinches off (1.0 V).
  3. Keep raising VDS past 1.0 V. The current stops rising, and the extra voltage appears across the depleted region at the drain.
  4. Double the overdrive (VGS 1.0 → 1.5 V). The saturation current rises 4×: the square law. Animation 7 shows why short channels give only 2×.

Check yourself

Why does the current saturate even though VDS keeps rising?Beyond pinch-off the voltage across the inverted part of the channel stays at VDS,sat = VGS − VT. The extra drain voltage is dropped across the short depleted gap at the drain, which electrons cross quickly in a strong field. The inverted channel, which sets the current, no longer sees it — in a long-channel device. Animation 6 shows what changes when the channel is short.
Pinch-off means zero electron charge at the drain end. How can current still flow?"Zero" is the gradual-channel approximation. The charge there is small but not zero, and the electrons move very fast in the high lateral field, so their product — the current — stays the same.
Below VT this model gives ID = 0. Is that true?No. A small current still flows by diffusion over the source barrier, falling about 10× for every 60–100 mV below threshold. In a chip with 1010 transistors this off-current adds up to watts; Animations 3 and 5 are about it.

The model behind it

Long-channel gradual-channel (square-law) model, with VT = 0.5 V and k = μnCoxW/L = 1 mA/V²:

ID = k[(VGS−VT)VDS − VDS²/2] for VDS < VGS−VT;   ID = (k/2)(VGS−VT)² beyond

The channel voltage profile solves the same current equation at each point: V(x) = Vov − √(Vov² − 2(x/L)F), with F = ID/k. Electron density along the channel is drawn proportional to Vov − V(x) and electron speed proportional to ID/Qn(x).

Left out: subthreshold current, channel-length modulation (Animation 6), velocity saturation (Animation 7) and body effect. The depletion depths are drawn qualitatively.
