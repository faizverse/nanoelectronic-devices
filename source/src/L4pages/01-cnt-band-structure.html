What you are seeing

Left. Near each corner of graphene's Brillouin zone (K and K′) the π bands form a cone, drawn here as contours: the darker the blue, the closer to zero energy. Rolling the sheet into a tube makes the wavefunction repeat around the circumference, so only wavevectors on parallel cutting lines survive, 2/d apart.

Right. Each cutting line is one subband of the tube. If a line passes exactly through K, two subbands cross at E = 0 and the tube is metallic. Otherwise the nearest line misses K by one third of the spacing, and a gap opens: Eg = 2accγ0/d.

More detail adds the density of states, whose sharp van Hove peaks sit at each subband edge, and the gap of every tube between 0.6 and 2.5 nm.

Check yourself

Why does a cutting line through K make the tube metallic?At K the π and π* bands of graphene touch. A subband that passes through K therefore contains states at E = 0 with no gap: two linear bands cross at the Fermi level.
Why does the gap of a semiconducting tube scale as 1/d?The lines are 2/d apart, so the nearest one misses K by 2/(3d). On the cone E = ħvFκ, so the band edge is at ħvF·2/(3d), and the gap is twice that: 4ħvF/3d = 2accγ0/d.
Why is the density of states of a metallic tube flat and non-zero at E = 0?Its two linear subbands have a constant slope, so dk/dE is constant: D = 8/hvF ≈ 2.2 states per eV per nm, including spin and the two valleys.

For the curious: the model behind it

Nearest-neighbour tight binding for graphene, E = ±γ0|1 + eik·a₁ + eik·a₂|, with acc = 0.142 nm and γ0 adjustable (2.7 eV by default). Zone folding: the allowed k = μK₁ + kK₂/|K₂|, μ = 0 … N − 1, with K₁ and K₂ from Saito, Dresselhaus and Dresselhaus. The density of states is a histogram of all subband energies, lightly smoothed.

Left out: curvature (which opens a small gap in non-armchair "metallic" tubes and changes the thinnest tubes), the overlap integral (which makes the bands slightly asymmetric) and excitons, which shift measured optical gaps.
