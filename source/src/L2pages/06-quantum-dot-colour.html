What you are seeing

Size sets the colour. When a dot absorbs ultraviolet light, an electron is lifted up and leaves a hole behind. The pair, called an exciton, then recombines and gives out light. In a small dot the electron and hole are squeezed tightly, like a particle in a small box, so their energies are pushed up and the light is bluer. In a large dot they are squeezed less and the light is redder.

The material sets the range. Each material has its own band gap, and no dot can glow below it. CdSe dots cover the visible colours. PbS dots of the same size glow in the infrared, which the eye cannot see.

Why it matters. Dots give pure colours in a narrow band, set by their size. That is why they are used in "QLED" and "QD-OLED" television screens, and to tag cells in different colours under a microscope.

Try this

  1. CdSe. Slide the diameter from 2 nm to 7 nm and call out the colours as they change.
  2. Which size gives the green of a screen, about 530 nm?
  3. Switch to PbS at the same size. Where has the light gone? Why, if the size is the same?
  4. Try InP, the cadmium-free material now used in screens. Can it make the same colours as CdSe?

Check yourself

Why does a smaller dot glow bluer?The smaller box squeezes the electron and hole more, pushing their energies up. More energy means bluer light.
Why does a 3 nm PbS dot glow in the infrared when a 3 nm CdSe dot glows green?PbS has a much smaller band gap. Size only sets the colour within the range the material allows.
Why do display makers want all their dots the same size?Dots of different sizes glow different colours, so a mix of sizes blurs the colour. "More detail" has a slider for the size spread.

For the curious: the model behind it

The Brus equation (1984) adds the trapping energy of the electron and hole, which grows as 1/R², and subtracts their attraction, which grows as 1/R:

E(R) = Eg + (ħ²π²/2R²)(1/me* + 1/mh*) − 1.8e²/(4πε0εrR)

It gets the trend right but overestimates the energy of small dots, so by default the page uses measured "sizing curves": CdSe and CdTe from Yu et al., Chem. Mater. 15, 2854 (2003); PbS from Moreels et al., ACS Nano 3, 3023 (2009). "More detail" lets you switch to Brus and compare. The light is drawn 30 meV below the first absorption.

CdSe: Eg 1.74 eV, me* 0.13, mh* 0.45, εr 10.6. CdTe: 1.50, 0.10, 0.40, 10.2. InP: 1.34, 0.077, 0.60, 12.5. PbS: 0.41, 0.085, 0.085, 17.2.

Left out: surface and ligand effects and fine structure. InP has no simple published sizing curve, so only Brus is used for it. Colours on screen are approximate.
