What you are seeing

Self-assembled (Stranski–Krastanov) dots. Deposit InAs on GaAs, atom layer by atom layer. InAs's natural lattice is 7% larger, so the film grows compressed to fit the substrate. Strain energy builds with each layer. At a critical thickness — about 1.7 monolayers for InAs on GaAs — it becomes cheaper for further material to gather into islands whose tops can relax. The islands, a few nanometres tall, are quantum dots; the thin film left underneath is the wetting layer. A smaller mismatch postpones the transition: Ge on Si (4%) needs about 3 monolayers.

Colloidal (hot-injection) dots. Inject precursors into a hot solvent and the dissolved "monomer" is suddenly far above its solubility. Once it passes a threshold, nuclei form in a burst, consuming monomer so fast that the concentration falls back below the threshold within moments: no new nuclei form after that, so all the dots are born together (LaMer). They then grow. While monomer is plentiful, small dots grow faster than large ones and the sizes focus. When monomer runs low, small dots dissolve to feed large ones (Ostwald ripening) and the sizes spread again — so growth is stopped by cooling, or quenching, at the right moment.

Try this in class

  1. Self-assembled, InAs/GaAs. Slowly raise the coverage. Ask the class to call out when islands appear (1.7 ML). What happens to the wetting layer after that?
  2. Switch to Ge/Si and repeat. Why does the transition come later?
  3. Colloidal. Press Inject and watch the concentration plot: a sharp burst of nucleation, then growth only. Watch the spread readout fall, then rise. Quench when it is smallest.
  4. Inject again with more precursor. Do you get more dots or bigger dots? Then raise the growth temperature: what changes?

Check yourself

Is Stranski–Krastanov growth top-down or bottom-up?Bottom-up. Nothing is patterned or removed; the atoms arrange themselves into islands because that lowers the strain energy.
Why must nucleation be short for uniform colloidal dots?Dots that nucleate later have less time to grow and end up smaller. A single short burst gives every dot the same start, and the growth stage can then narrow the distribution further.
Why do small dots dissolve during Ostwald ripening?Atoms on a strongly curved surface are less tightly bound, so a small dot's equilibrium solubility is higher (the Gibbs–Thomson effect). When the monomer concentration falls below that, small dots lose atoms to the solution while larger ones still gain them.

The model behind it

SK: the wetting layer grows to a critical thickness hc ≈ 12.2/ε ML (ε, the mismatch in per cent; this gives 1.7 ML for InAs/GaAs, 2.9 ML for Ge/Si); further material goes into islands with a fixed aspect ratio. Colloidal: nucleation rate J ∝ exp(−B/ln²S) for supersaturation S; diffusion-limited growth with the Gibbs–Thomson correction,

dr/dt = G (S − eα/r) / r,   with S falling as monomer is consumed

Both models are qualitative. Island density, size and shape depend on temperature and growth rate in ways not modelled, and the colloidal time scale is illustrative: real syntheses take seconds to tens of minutes.
