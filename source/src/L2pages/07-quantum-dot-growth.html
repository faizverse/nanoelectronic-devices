What you are seeing

Self-assembled dots. Atoms of InAs land on a GaAs crystal, one layer at a time. InAs atoms are naturally a little further apart (about 7%), so the film is squeezed to fit, like a carpet slightly too big for its room. The first layer lies flat: the wetting layer. After about 1.7 layers of atoms the squeeze is too much, and new material bunches into small islands where it can spread out. The islands are quantum dots. No pattern is drawn: the dots build themselves.

Dots from a flask. Inject the ingredients into a hot liquid and a sudden burst of tiny seed crystals forms, all at the same moment. After that no new seeds form; the existing ones just grow. Because they all started together, they stay nearly the same size, and as they grow their colour moves from blue towards red. Cooling the flask ("Quench") stops the growth at the colour you want.

Try this

  1. Self-assembled. Slowly add material. Call out when the islands appear (about 1.7 layers).
  2. Keep adding. Does the wetting layer get any thicker after the islands appear?
  3. Switch to the flask and press Inject. Watch the dots grow and change colour.
  4. Press Quench when the dots glow green. Then inject again and stop at red. Which took longer?

Check yourself

Is self-assembled (Stranski–Krastanov) growth top-down or bottom-up?Bottom-up. Nothing is drawn or removed: the atoms gather into islands by themselves, because that relieves the squeeze.
Why do the flask-grown dots come out nearly the same size?They are all seeded in one short burst and then grow together, so they all have the same time to grow.
How do you get red dots instead of green from the same recipe?Let them grow longer before stopping the growth. Bigger dots glow redder.

For the curious: the model behind it

Self-assembled: the wetting layer grows to a critical thickness hc ≈ 12.2/ε layers (ε, the mismatch in per cent), which gives 1.7 for InAs/GaAs and 2.9 for Ge/Si; further material goes into islands. Flask: seeds form at a rate J ∝ exp(−B/ln²S) (B = 90, a sharp threshold near S = 4.4), where S is how far the dissolved building blocks exceed their saturation level, and each dot grows as

dr/dt = G (S − eα/r) / r,   with S falling as material is used up

Small dots dissolve when S runs low (Ostwald ripening), so the size spread first narrows, then widens. "More detail" shows the materials, the ingredient amount and temperature, and graphs of where the material goes, the supersaturation and the size distribution. Colours use the CdSe sizing curve of Animation 6.

Both models are qualitative. Island size and density depend on temperature and growth rate in ways not modelled, and the flask time scale is illustrative.
