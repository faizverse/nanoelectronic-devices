What you are seeing

Only standing waves fit. An electron trapped between two walls must be a standing wave, so only certain wavelengths, and therefore certain energies, are allowed. With infinitely high walls the wave must vanish at the walls and exactly n half-wavelengths fit:

En = ħ²π²n² / (2m*L²)  ·  n = 1, 2, 3, …

Halving the width raises every level fourfold; a lighter effective mass raises them too. The lowest level is never zero: confining the electron gives it a minimum kinetic energy, the zero-point energy.

Real walls are finite. In a GaAs well between AlGaAs barriers the walls are only about 0.24 eV high. The wavefunction leaks into the barriers, decaying as e−κ|x|, so the electron behaves as if the box were wider and every level sits lower than the infinite-well estimate. Only a limited number of states are bound; above the barrier top the electron is free.

The readouts compare the level spacing with kBT = 25.9 meV: quantisation shows up at room temperature only when the spacing is several times larger.

Try this in class

  1. Infinite walls, GaAs, L = 10 nm. Read E1 (56 meV) and E2 (225 meV). Ask the class to predict E1 at 5 nm before you move the slider (four times larger: 225 meV).
  2. Switch to finite walls, 0.24 eV. E1 drops to 32 meV and only three states remain bound. Why is the infinite well so far off?
  3. Show |ψ|² and narrow the well to 3 nm. Watch the "outside" probability on the right climb: most of the electron is now in the barrier.
  4. Change the mass from GaAs to silicon's heavy valley (0.92 m0). How narrow must the well be before E2 − E1 exceeds 3kBT?

Check yourself

Why is n = 0 not allowed?With n = 0 the infinite-well wavefunction is sin(0) = 0 everywhere: there is no electron. The boundary conditions exclude it; the uncertainty principle explains why the lowest allowed energy must be above zero.
|ψ|² at the centre of the n = 1 state is about 2/L. Is that the probability of finding the electron there?No. |ψ|² is a probability density, with units of 1/length. The probability of finding the electron in a small interval Δx is |ψ|²Δx; at an exact point it is zero.
Why do all finite-well levels sit below the infinite-well levels?The wavefunction leaks into the barriers, so it fits a longer wavelength than the well width alone allows. A longer wavelength means a smaller momentum and a lower kinetic energy.
A 10 nm well has E2 − E1 = 92 meV with finite walls. What light does the 1→2 transition absorb?λ = 1240/92 nm ≈ 13.5 µm, in the mid-infrared. This intersubband transition is the basis of quantum-well infrared detectors and quantum cascade lasers (Document 3).

The model behind it

Infinite well: En = ħ²π²n²/(2m*L²), ψn = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L). Finite well: the even and odd matching conditions

k tan(kL/2) = κ  (even),   −k cot(kL/2) = κ  (odd),   k = √(2m*E)/ħ, κ = √(2m*(V0−E))/ħ

are solved by bisection, and the exact normalised wavefunctions are drawn. The "outside" probability is ∫|ψ|² over the barriers.

Left out: the change of effective mass at the interface (the BenDaniel–Duke condition), band non-parabolicity, and strain. With the same mass inside and out the GaAs levels are slightly low; the trend and orders of magnitude are right.
