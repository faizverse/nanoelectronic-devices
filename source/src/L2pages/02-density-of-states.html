What you are seeing

Counting states. The density of states g(E) is the number of electron states per unit energy. In bulk material the electron is free in three directions and g(E) rises as √E from the band edge.

Each confined direction reshapes it. Confine the electron in one direction (a quantum well) and each confined level becomes the bottom of a 2-D subband with a constant density of states: g(E) becomes a staircase. Confine in two directions (a wire) and each subband gives a 1/√(E−En) spike. Confine in all three (a dot) and only discrete levels remain, like an atom.

Why it matters. The electrons fill these states according to the Boltzmann factor e−E/kBT. The rust area shows where they actually are. The more directions are confined, the narrower the energy range the electrons occupy — which is why quantum-well lasers need less current than bulk lasers, and why quantum-dot lasers hardly change with temperature. Real dots differ in size, so their sharp lines blur; the size-spread slider shows that.

Try this in class

  1. Start with Bulk. Note the energy spread of the electrons (about 1.8 kBT wide). Ask: what happens to it as we confine?
  2. Switch to Well, 10 nm. The first step sits 56 meV above the band edge, and the electrons pile up just above it. Compare the spread.
  3. Wire, then Dot. Watch the spikes and lines appear and the occupied region shrink. At what size does almost every electron sit in the lowest level?
  4. Dot, size spread 10%. The lines broaden to tens of meV — the "inhomogeneous broadening" of a real quantum-dot laser. Now cool to 77 K: which structure changes most?

Check yourself

Why is the 2-D density of states constant within a subband?In two free dimensions the number of states inside a circle of radius k grows as k², and E ∝ k², so the number grows linearly with E. Its derivative — the density of states — is constant: m*/πħ² per unit area, including spin.
Is a quantum dot 1-D?No. A dot is zero-dimensional: confined in all three directions, with no direction of free motion. The dimension counts the free directions — well 2-D, wire 1-D, dot 0-D.
Why do the wire spikes not become infinite in a real wire?Scattering, disorder and finite length broaden every state, which rounds off the 1/√(E − En) divergence. The model adds a small broadening for the same reason.

The model behind it

GaAs-like electrons with effective mass m*, confined by infinite walls of width L in each confined direction, so the subband edges are sums of ħ²π²n²/(2m*L²). Per subband:

g3D ∝ √E,   g2D = m*/πħ² for E > En,   g1D ∝ 1/√(E − En),   g0D = 2Σ δ(E − En)

Occupation is non-degenerate (Boltzmann), as for injected carriers in a laser at low density. The spread readout is the full width at half maximum of g(E)·f(E). Curves are normalised to the same peak height so their shapes can be compared.

Left out: finite barriers (Animation 1 shows their effect), valence bands, many-body effects and degenerate filling at high density.
