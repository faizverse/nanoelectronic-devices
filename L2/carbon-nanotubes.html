What you are seeing

The chiral vector. A single-walled nanotube is a sheet of graphene rolled into a seamless cylinder. The roll is fixed by the lattice vector that wraps once around the circumference, C = na1 + ma2. The pair of integers (n, m) determines everything: diameter, twist and electronic character. Tubes with m = 0 are zigzag, those with n = m armchair, the rest chiral.

d = (a/π)√(n² + nm + m²),   a = 0.246 nm

Metal or semiconductor. Rolling makes the electron's wave periodic around the circumference, which allows only certain lines of states through graphene's band structure. If n − m is divisible by 3, one of those lines passes through the point where graphene's bands touch and the tube is a metal. Otherwise it is a semiconductor with a band gap that falls as 1/d — about 0.8 eV·nm / d.

Two-thirds of all possible tubes are semiconducting. A transistor needs a semiconducting tube; a metallic one cannot be switched off. Growth produces a mixture, which is why sorting tubes is the central problem of nanotube electronics.

Try this in class

  1. Start at (10, 0), a zigzag tube. Semiconductor or metal? Predict from n − m, then check. Now try (9, 0).
  2. Set (10, 10), armchair. Every armchair tube is metallic. What is its diameter (1.36 nm) and its ballistic resistance?
  3. Find two semiconducting tubes with nearly the same diameter but different (n, m). Do their gaps agree with the 0.8/d rule?
  4. Pick tubes at random (the Random button) and count how many would make a working transistor out of ten.

Check yourself

Why does the gap fall as the tube gets wider?The allowed lines through graphene's band structure are spaced by 2/d in wavevector. In a wider tube they lie closer together, so the nearest line passes closer to the point where the bands touch, and the gap shrinks in proportion to 1/d.
A metallic tube is ballistic over a micrometre. Is its resistance zero?No. With two conducting modes, each spin-degenerate, a perfect ballistic tube still has h/4e² ≈ 6.45 kΩ, set by the contacts. Ballistic means no resistance that grows with length.
Why is a chip of a billion CNTFETs so hard to make?One metallic tube shorts a transistor, so semiconducting purity must exceed about 99.9999%. Tubes must also be placed, aligned and contacted at uniform density — problems of manufacturing, not of physics.

The model behind it

Lattice vectors a1, a2 of length a = 0.246 nm at 60°, carbon–carbon distance acc = 0.142 nm. Chiral angle θ = arctan[√3m/(2n + m)]. Translational vector T = [(2m + n)a1 − (2n + m)a2]/dR, dR = gcd(2n + m, 2m + n). Semiconducting gap from the zone-folding tight-binding model:

Eg ≈ 2accγ0/d,   γ0 ≈ 2.9 eV

Left out: curvature effects, which open a small gap (tens of meV) in non-armchair "metallic" tubes and shift the gaps of the smallest tubes; and excitonic effects in optical spectra. Diameters below about 0.6 nm are strained and unstable.
