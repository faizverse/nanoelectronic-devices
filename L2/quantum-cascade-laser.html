What you are seeing

A staircase instead of a cliff. In an ordinary laser an electron makes one big drop, meets a hole, gives out one photon and is gone. A quantum cascade laser uses only electrons. It is a stack of many thin quantum wells, and the applied voltage tilts the stack into a staircase.

A photon at every step. At each step the electron drops from a higher trapped level (rust) to a lower one (blue) and gives out a photon. It is not destroyed: it moves on through a thin wall, by tunnelling, into the top of the next step, and drops again. One electron can give out a photon at every step.

The steps must line up. With too little voltage the way into the next step (green) sits too low; with too much it sits too high. Only when it lines up with the top of the next step do electrons flow and the laser switch on. The step size, set by the thickness of the wells, sets the colour: infrared light, used for sensing tiny traces of gases.

Try this

  1. Start at zero voltage and raise it slowly. Watch the electrons stall, then flow once the green and rust levels line up.
  2. Keep raising the voltage. What happens to the laser when the levels pass each other?
  3. Make the active well wider. Does the light's wavelength get longer or shorter? You will need a different voltage to line the steps up again.

Check yourself

Why is a quantum cascade laser called unipolar?It uses only electrons. No holes are needed, because every drop happens between two levels that electrons occupy.
What sets the colour of its light?The size of each step, which is set by the thickness of the wells, not by the band gap of the material.
Does "one electron, many photons" make it very powerful?It gives more light for each electron, but each photon carries little energy and the laser needs a large current, so it runs hot. Its real strength is the infrared wavelengths it reaches.

For the curious: the model behind it

Each step's active region is one finite well (In0.53Ga0.47As in Al0.48In0.52As: m* = 0.043 m0, V0 = 0.52 eV). The photon energy is E2 − E1. The electron then loses one optical-phonon energy (34 meV) plus 60 meV in the injector, so the steps line up when the voltage per stage is

Vstage = (E2 − E1) + 0.034 + 0.060 eV

The tunnelling current is a resonance 20 meV wide around alignment; the laser turns on above a threshold current. "More detail" shows the current and light against voltage, the alignment, and the number of stages.

Left out: real designs use two or three coupled wells per step and a graded injector. Currents and light are in relative units.
