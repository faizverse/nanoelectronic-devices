What you are seeing

In saturation most of the drain voltage drops across the short pinch-off region at the drain, so the lateral field there peaks at several 10⁵ V/cm. An electron gains energy qΔV for every volt it falls through without colliding. Most collide every ~10 nm and stay cool; a few lucky electrons run further and get hot.

  • Above about 1.5 eV (the 1.1 eV gap plus momentum conservation) an electron can create an electron–hole pair by impact ionisation. The hole flows to the body as substrate current.
  • Above about 3.1 eV (the Si/SiO₂ barrier for electrons) it can be injected into the oxide and trapped, shifting VT over the device lifetime.

The substrate current peaks at roughly VGS ≈ VDS/2: at low gate voltage there are few electrons; at high gate voltage VDS,sat rises and the peak field falls. Because both effects come from the same hot electrons, Isub is the standard monitor for hot-carrier damage.

The modern twist: no electron can gain more energy than qVDS from the supply. At 0.7 V there are no 1.5 eV or 3.1 eV electrons. Hot-carrier damage today comes from many moderately energetic carriers breaking interface bonds cumulatively — not shown in this model.

Try this in class

  1. Start with the 1990 · 5 V preset. Watch electrons turn gold and red near the drain, and holes drop into the substrate. Count the oxide injections (rare — that is the point: lifetime is years, not seconds).
  2. Sweep VGS and find the peak of Isub. Compare with VDS/2.
  3. Turn on the lightly doped drain (LDD). The field peak spreads and flattens; read the change in Isub and lifetime. This is why LDD was invented.
  4. Switch to Today · 0.7 V. The peak field is still high (the channel is tiny), yet nothing gets hot. Ask the class why: energy, not field, is the limit.

Check yourself

The peak field in the 0.7 V device is similar to the 5 V device. Why no impact ionisation?The field is high but the region is only ~10 nm long and the total potential drop is at most 0.7 V. An electron's energy is bounded by the voltage it has fallen through, so it can never reach 1.5 eV.
Why does substrate current matter beyond reliability?Holes flowing through the body's resistance raise the local body potential. Near 0.6 V the source–body junction forward-biases and the parasitic npn bipolar turns on; with avalanche feedback this causes snapback breakdown. In SOI and FinFETs the body floats, so even small hole currents shift the body potential (floating-body effect).
How did the LDD help, and what did it cost?A lightly doped region at the drain edge spreads the voltage drop over a longer distance, lowering the peak field. The cost is extra series resistance, which lowers drive current.

The model behind it

Velocity-saturated channel (Animation 7) with a pinch-off region of characteristic length l ≈ 0.22 · tox1/3 · xj1/2 (cm) in which V(x) = VDS,sat + lEc·sinh((x − xp)/l), so the peak field is Em = √(Ec² + ((VDS − VDS,sat)/l)²). Substrate current uses the classic form

Isub/ID = (Ai/Bi)(VDS − VDS,sat) exp(−Bi/Em) · S(VDS)

with Bi = 1.7 MV/cm, Ai/Bi = 0.3 V⁻¹ and an energy cut-off S = 1/(1 + exp((1.5 − VDS)/0.08)). Relative lifetime follows Hu's empirical law τ ∝ (W/ID)(Isub/ID)−2.9, referenced to the 1990 preset at 5 V. The particles are a lucky-electron Monte Carlo: energy gained equals potential fallen through since the last collision (mean free path 9.2 nm).

Representative constants; LDD is modelled only as l × 2.5. Bias-temperature instability, now usually the leading wear-out mechanism, is not included.
