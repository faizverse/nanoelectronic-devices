Drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL)

What you are seeing

The left plot is the energy an electron needs to travel from source (left) to drain (right). The hump in the middle is the source–channel barrier. The gate sets its height; current is electrons hopping over it, so the current depends on the barrier height Eb exponentially:

ID ∝ exp(−Eb / kT)  ⇒  60 meV lower barrier ≈ 10× more current at 300 K

In a long channel the top of the barrier is flat and only the gate can move it. In a short channel the drain's field reaches the barrier top: raise VDS and the barrier falls. The gate then needs less voltage to turn the device on, so VT drops with drain voltage — the parallel shift between the two curves on the right.

The figure of merit is DIBL = −ΔVT/ΔVDS in mV/V. Well-scaled devices sit at 20–60 mV/V; above about 100 mV/V the gate has lost control.

Try this in class

  1. Planar bulk, L = 100 nm, VGS = 0. Sweep VDS from 0.05 to 1 V. The barrier top does not move and DIBL is close to zero. Ask: why doesn't the drain matter here?
  2. Reduce L to about 40 nm (≈ 5λ) and sweep again. The barrier now falls by tens of meV; DIBL is around 70 mV/V.
  3. Go to 25 nm. The barrier top is pulled low and pushed toward the source, and electrons pour over it. Read the off-current.
  4. Keep L = 25 nm and switch to FinFET, then GAA. The natural length λ shrinks and DIBL recovers — the reason the industry changed the geometry (Document 3, Section 6).
  5. Ask the class to predict how much the current rises when the barrier drops by 120 meV, then check it against the electrons crossing (≈ 100×).

Check yourself

Why does DIBL shift the transfer curve sideways rather than change its slope?In subthreshold the drain lowers the barrier by roughly the same amount at every gate voltage. The gate therefore needs a fixed amount less voltage to reach the same barrier height: a parallel shift. A change of slope is a different symptom — swing degradation, Animation 5.
Why does the barrier top move toward the source at high drain bias?The drain's influence decays over a distance of order λ from the drain end. Raising VDS pulls the right-hand side of the hump down, so the highest remaining point slides toward the source. Injection over that point is what sets the current.
A device has DIBL = 150 mV/V and SS = 90 mV/dec. How much larger is IOFF at VDS = 0.7 V than at 0.05 V?ΔVT = 0.65 V × 150 mV/V ≈ 98 mV. That is 98/90 ≈ 1.1 decades, so about 12× more off-current.

The model behind it

The surface potential along the channel is the standard quasi-two-dimensional (scale-length) solution of Poisson's equation:

ψ(x) = ψL + (Vbi − ψL) sinh((L−x)/λ)/sinh(L/λ) + (Vbi + VDS − ψL) sinh(x/λ)/sinh(L/λ)

with λ = √( (εSi/εox) tSi tox / N ) and N = 1, 2, 4 for bulk, FinFET and GAA, as in Document 3, Section 1.1. ψL is the long-channel potential the gate would set. VT is the gate voltage at which the barrier top reaches its long-channel threshold value. Current uses an interpolation that is exponential below VT and limited by velocity saturation well above it.

Parameters: EOT 1 nm; body 20 nm (bulk depletion depth), 6 nm (fin), 5 nm (sheet); Vbi = 1.1 V; long-channel VT = 0.30 V. No halo doping, quantum confinement or series resistance. Trends and orders of magnitude are right; absolute values are illustrative.
