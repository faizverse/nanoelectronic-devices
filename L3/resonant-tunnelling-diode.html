Resonant-tunnelling diode

What you are seeing

A sharp filter. Two AlGaAs barriers a couple of nanometres thick enclose a GaAs quantum well with one allowed level (green). Electrons whose energy matches that level pass through both barriers almost every time; all others are almost always reflected. The blue curve on the right shows this: near 100% at the level's energy, tiny elsewhere.

The voltage moves the level. The voltage tilts the picture and pulls the level down. When it lines up with the electrons waiting in the emitter (left), they pour through: the current peaks. When it drops below the bottom of the emitter's band, no electron has the right energy and the current falls. That fall is negative differential resistance.

Watch the electrons. Electrons arrive at many energies. Those at the right energy go straight through; the rest bounce back.

Try this

  1. Raise the voltage slowly from zero and stop at the peak. Where is the green level compared with the electrons on the left?
  2. Go past the peak. Why does the current fall?
  3. Make the well narrower. Does the peak move to a higher or lower voltage? Why? (Think of Lecture 2: smaller box, higher level.)
  4. Make the barriers thicker. What happens to the sharpness of the blue peak, and to the current?
  5. In More detail, compare 77 K and 300 K. Why is the valley lower when cold?

Check yourself

How can an electron cross two barriers almost perfectly, when it rarely crosses one?At the level's energy, the waves bouncing back and forth between the two barriers add up in step, like a string driven at its own note. The reflections from the two barriers cancel, so the wave passes through. At other energies they do not cancel and almost all of the wave is reflected.
Why does a narrower well move the peak to a higher voltage?A narrower well raises its allowed level (energy grows as 1/L²). The voltage must then pull it down further before it lines up with the emitter's electrons.
Why is a resonant-tunnelling diode so fast?An electron spends only a very short time in the well, a fraction of a picosecond (shown in More detail as the level's lifetime), and the device is small with a tiny capacitance. RTD oscillators have reached about 2 THz.

For the curious: the model behind it

The conduction-band profile (barriers 0.33 eV high, effective mass 0.067 m0 throughout) is cut into thin slices, with the applied voltage dropping linearly across the undoped region (the barriers, the well and 2 nm spacers). The transmission T(E, V) is calculated exactly with transfer matrices. The current is the Tsu–Esaki integral J ∝ ∫ T(E, V) N(E, V) dE, where N(E, V) = kBT ln[(1 + e(EF−E)/kBT)/(1 + e(EF−E−qV)/kBT)] counts the electrons available, with the emitter Fermi level 40 meV above its band edge. The level's lifetime is ħ/Γ, with Γ the width of the transmission peak.

Left out: charge building up in the well, scattering, band bending in the contacts and series resistance. Real devices show the same shape; their peak voltages are higher because part of the voltage drops elsewhere.
